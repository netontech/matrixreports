Attendance reporting · findings

The report can show five breaks. The doors recorded twelve.

The attendance data is complete. The sheet that reads it is not — and because hours worked are calculated from what the sheet can show, every break it cannot fit is silently paid as time at work.

Source Matrix COSEC, 119,800 punch records Period 12,261 attendance days Method raw punches re-paired in code

The shape of the evidence

Start with the existing sheet for a single day. Of 37 staff, the number recorded as stepping out looks like this: six at zero, one at one, one at two, seven at three, five at four — and then seventeen at exactly five, with nothing above it.

That is not how people behave. A real distribution of how often someone leaves the floor tapers off at the top. This one falls to five at four breaks, then more than triples at five and stops dead. A distribution that rises into its maximum and terminates there is not observed data — it is censored data, and the maximum is the censor.

What the punches actually show

Re-paired from the raw door records, with no limit on how many times a person may step out, the true distribution appears — and it behaves properly, peaking at four and tapering away.

Why five, exactly

The limit is not a policy or a setting. It is the width of a table. The daily summary the report reads stores a day as twelve fixed columns, Punch1 through Punch12, and twelve columns hold exactly one arrival, five round trips, and one departure.

Why it overstates hours

A missing column would be a cosmetic problem. This one is arithmetic. On every row of the existing sheet, Total Out Minutes is the sum of the break cells it managed to display, and hours worked is derived from it:

Actual Works Hours = (Last Out − 1st In) − Total Out Minutes

A sixth break never enters Total Out Minutes, so it is never subtracted. The overstatement is not a side effect of the limit — it follows from it by arithmetic.

On the sampled day, nine of thirty-five staff had taken more breaks than the sheet could hold. For one of them the gap is not marginal:

Two breaks — one of two hours forty-nine minutes, one of two hours — fell past the fifth slot. Neither was subtracted. The day was paid as if nearly four hours of absence were time at work.

A second, unrelated error

Durations are stored as hh.mm and then added up

Throughout the weekly and monthly sheets a duration is written as a number whose whole part is hours and whose decimal part is minutes — 8.14 meaning eight hours fourteen minutes — and those numbers are then summed. The decimal part counts to a hundred while minutes count to sixty, so every total is wrong, and the error compounds with each cell added.

It also corrupts single days. One employee's break time reads 1.04 — one hour four minutes. The report deducted four minutes. A full hour was credited as work, on a day that was never truncated at all.

This is worth separating from the ceiling: fixing the column limit does not fix this, and fixing this does not fix the ceiling. They are two defects that happen to push the same direction.

What changes

The punches are read directly and paired in code, where nothing is fixed at twelve. A day with twenty-four punches produces twelve sessions and eleven breaks, and the sheet grows an OUT / IN / MINS group for each one. A quiet day still renders at the familiar five, so the report looks like the one people already read.

Arrival and departure times are unchanged — they matched the existing report on thirty-four of thirty-five staff, and the two that differed were both errors in the old sheet: one took an exit swipe as the day's first arrival, the other treated an unclosed entry as a departure. Both are now flagged rather than quietly assumed.

Durations are kept as real time values and displayed as HH:MM, so they add up correctly in the sheet and do not wrap at twenty-four hours.

Figures are aggregates drawn from the attendance database and the existing workbooks. No individual is named.
